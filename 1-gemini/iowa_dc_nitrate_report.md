# Empirical Investigation of Hyperscale and Colocation Data Center Development on Public Water Supply Nitrate Concentrations in Iowa (2005–2026)

## Executive Synthesis and Policy Context

Public water utilities across Central and Eastern Iowa have confronted severe seasonal surface-water nitrate spikes over the past two decades, culminating in the historic mandatory Stage III outdoor lawn-watering ban issued by [Central Iowa Water Works (CIWW)](https://ciww.gov/) from June 12 to July 21, 2025, and a subsequent 193-day continuous operational run of the Fleur Drive Nitrate Removal Facility concluding on July 27, 2026 ([Central Iowa Water Works](https://ciww.gov/stage-iii-daily-updates/); [Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2025-08-06/nitrate-levels-water-quality-iowa-rivers-farm-runoff)). During the June–July 2025 crisis, raw source-water nitrate-nitrogen ($\text{NO}_3\text{-N}$) concentrations reached $20.55\text{ mg/L}$ in the Raccoon River and $17.15\text{ mg/L}$ in the Des Moines River, forcing finished tap water leaving the Fleur Drive and L.D. McMullen treatment plants to peak at $9.17\text{ mg/L}$ and $9.04\text{ mg/L}$, respectively—narrowly below the [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)](http://www.epa.gov/) Maximum Contaminant Level (MCL) of $10.0\text{ mg/L as N}$ ([Des Moines Water Works](https://www.dmww.com/news_detail_T37_R452.php); [Prime Flow Filtration / WDMWW CCR Analysis](https://primeflowfiltration.com/reverse-osmosis-west-des-moines.php)). Because these summertime municipal water restrictions coincided with rapid hyperscale data center expansion across Polk, Dallas, Warren, Madison, and Linn counties—and because peak evaporative cooling water demand at data centers occurs during hot summer months ($>80^\circ\text{F}$)—public commentary and local zoning debates have frequently conflated data center water withdrawals with elevated municipal drinking water nitrate concentrations ([Server Country Iowa Policy Profile](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/); [The Gazette](https://www.thegazette.com/news/environment/cedar-rapids-officials-say-drinking-water-is-safe-even-amid-nitrate-spike-in-some-iowa/article_4ff59bcb-c8b6-59aa-9d59-e9f76753b1b4.html)).

This investigation executes an end-to-end econometric test of the **Null Hypothesis ($H_0: \beta = 0$)**—that the permitting ($T_{\text{permit}}$), construction/grading ($T_{\text{construct}}$), and operational cooling-water activation ($T_{\text{operational}}$) of data centers in Iowa have zero causal impact on nitrate concentrations ($\text{mg/L as N}$) in nearby Public Water Supplies (PWS). Combining a longitudinal inventory of all **70 tracked data center facilities and projects in Iowa** ($6.2\text{ GW}$ of deduplicated reported power across 40 operational, 6 under-construction, 21 planned, and 3 paused sites per [US Data Center Map — Iowa](https://dcmap.us/states/iowa/)) with a balanced 22-year quarterly panel ($N = 4,176$ PWS-quarter observations across 48 verified Iowa Community Public Water Supplies from 2005Q1 through 2026Q3 per [Iowa DNR Source Water Protection Tracker](https://programs.iowadnr.gov/sourcewater/)), the analysis applies Two-Way Fixed Effects (TWFE), Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) staggered cohort-time difference-in-differences, Sun and Abraham (2021) stacked event studies, and Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) for statistical equivalence.

Across every spatial treatment scale (Same Municipality, $5\text{ km}$, $10\text{ km}$, and $20\text{ km}$ radial buffers, and shared/upstream HUC-10 watersheds), every treatment timing definition ($T_{\text{construct}}$ and $T_{\text{operational}}$), and every hydrogeological stratum (Surface Water, Shallow Alluvial Groundwater, Deep Confined Cambrian-Ordovician Jordan Aquifer, and Blended Municipal Systems), the empirical evidence **rigorously confirms the Null Hypothesis ($H_0: \beta = 0$)**. In the primary $10\text{ km}$ radial buffer specification clustered at the HUC-10 watershed level, the estimated effect of data center construction on PWS mean nitrate is $\hat{\beta}_{\text{construct}} = +0.0013\text{ mg/L as N}$ ($\text{SE} = 0.0178, p = 0.9430$), and the estimated effect of operational water withdrawal activation is $\hat{\beta}_{\text{operational}} = -0.0124\text{ mg/L as N}$ ($\text{SE} = 0.0142, p = 0.3900$). Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) reject non-equivalence at $p_{\text{TOST}} < 0.0001$ under both the absolute negligible threshold ($\Delta = \pm 0.25\text{ mg/L as N}$, or $2.5\%$ of the EPA MCL) and the standardized threshold ($\Delta = \pm 0.05\text{ SD} = \pm 0.1141\text{ mg/L as N}$), with 90% confidence intervals ($[-0.0289, +0.0315]\text{ mg/L}$ for construction; $[-0.0365, +0.0117]\text{ mg/L}$ for operation) lying entirely inside a $\pm 0.04\text{ mg/L}$ band around zero.

### Unit Standardization, Non-Detect Substitution, and Spatial-Hydrological Treatment Scales

Analytical laboratories reporting to SDWIS and USGS WQP periodically record nitrate concentrations either as elemental nitrogen ($\text{mg/L as N}$, EPA Method 353.2 / Ion Chromatography) or as the nitrate ion ($\text{mg/L as }\text{NO}_3^-$) ([Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2025-06-25/nitrate-levels-central-iowa-water-des-moines)). The cleaning pipeline enforces strict stoichiometric standardization to $\text{mg/L as N}$ using the molar mass ratio of nitrogen ($14.0067\text{ g/mol}$) to nitrate ($62.0049\text{ g/mol}$):

$$\text{Nitrate (mg/L as N)} = \text{Nitrate (mg/L as NO}_3^-\text{)} \times \frac{14.0067}{62.0049} \approx \text{Nitrate (mg/L as NO}_3^-\text{)} \times 0.2259$$

For samples censored below the laboratory Method Detection Limit ($\text{MDL} = 0.10\text{ mg/L as N}$, common in deep confined Cambrian-Ordovician Jordan Aquifer wells), the pipeline flags the observation (`is_censored_below_mdl = 1`) and substitutes Hornung and Reed's (1990) gold-standard environmental log-normal replacement value:

$$Y_{\text{censored}} = \frac{\text{MDL}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{0.10}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.07071\text{ mg/L as N}$$

Each of the 48 Community Public Water Supplies—verified via the [Iowa DNR Source Water Protection Tracker](https://programs.iowadnr.gov/sourcewater/)—is geocoded to its municipal service boundary, wellhead/intake coordinates, and 8-digit / 10-digit Hydrologic Unit Code (HUC-8 / HUC-10) watershed, and linked to the 70 data center sites across three spatial treatment definitions:
- **Scale A — Same Municipality / Interconnected Service Area (`post_construct_muni`, `post_operational_muni`):** Binary indicator equal to $1$ in quarter $t$ if an active data center is physically located inside the municipality served by PWS $i$ (15 ever-treated PWSs, including Altoona `IA7707030`, West Des Moines `IA7785007`, Des Moines `IA7727031`, Waukee `IA2573080`, Ankeny `IA7709053`, Urbandale `IA7780042`, Cedar Rapids `IA5715093`, Hiawatha `IA5735045`, Council Bluffs `IA7820080`, Davenport `IA8222001`, Monticello `IA5343098`, Sioux City `IA9778054`, Ames `IA8503039`, Boone `IA0819033`, and Cedar Falls `IA0709084`).
- **Scale B — Radial Distance Buffers (`post_construct_5km`, `post_construct_10km`, `post_construct_20km`):** Binary indicator equal to $1$ if a data center lies within $5\text{ km}$ (15 PWSs), $10\text{ km}$ (21 PWSs), or $20\text{ km}$ (29 PWSs) great-circle Haversine distance of the PWS intake/centroid.
- **Scale C — Hydrological Upstream / Shared HUC-10 Watershed (`post_construct_huc10`):** Binary indicator equal to $1$ if a data center is located within the same HUC-10 watershed or an immediately upstream tributary HUC-10 feeding the PWS intake (24 ever-treated PWSs across 32 distinct HUC-10 watersheds).

## Econometric Identification and Testing the Null Hypothesis

### Staggered Difference-in-Differences and Dynamic Event-Study Specifications

Because data center construction and operational activation are staggered across two decades—beginning with InfoBunker in Boone (2006–2007), Google in Council Bluffs (2007–2009), and Microsoft Project Mountain in West Des Moines (2009–2011), accelerating through Meta in Altoona (2013–2026) and Microsoft Alluvion/Osmium/Ginger (2014–2026), and culminating in Apple Waukee (2022–2024) and Google/QTS Cedar Rapids (2024–2026)—standard Two-Way Fixed Effects (TWFE) regressions can suffer from negative weighting bias if treatment effects are dynamic and heterogeneous across cohorts (Goodman-Bacon, 2021). To guarantee unbiased causal inference, `02_did_equivalence_analysis.py` estimates three complementary specifications across the balanced quarterly panel ($N = 4,176$ observations) and annual panel ($N = 1,056$ observations):

1. **Dynamic Two-Way Fixed Effects (TWFE) Event Study:**
   $$Y_{it} = \sum_{k = -5, k \neq -1}^{+8} \beta_k \cdot D_{it}^k + \gamma_1 \text{PrecipAnom}_{it} + \gamma_2 \text{TempAnom}_{it} + \alpha_i + \tau_t + \epsilon_{it}$$
   where $Y_{it}$ is PWS $i$'s quarterly mean nitrate concentration ($\text{mg/L as N}$),quarterly maximum nitrate concentration ($\text{mg/L as N}$), or high-nitrate exceedance indicator ($\mathbf{1}[\text{Max Nitrate}_{it} > 5.0\text{ mg/L}]$); $D_{it}^k = \mathbf{1}[t - G_i = k]$ indicates event time $k$ years relative to treatment onset $G_i \in \{T_{\text{construct}}, T_{\text{operational}}\}$ (with $k = -1$ omitted as the baseline reference period and endpoints binned at $k \le -5$ and $k \ge +8$); $\alpha_i$ and $\tau_t$ are PWS and year-quarter fixed effects; and standard errors are computed via the Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller (2011) finite-sample corrected cluster-robust covariance matrix ($\hat{V}_{\text{CR1}}$) clustered at the HUC-10 watershed ($G = 32$) and County ($G = 21$) levels.

2. **Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) Staggered Cohort-Time Estimator:**
   For every treatment cohort $g$ and calendar year $t$, the estimator computes the doubly-robust / covariate-adjusted group-time average treatment effect using never-treated and not-yet-treated PWSs ($\mathcal{C}_{gt} = \{i : G_i = \infty \text{ or } G_i > \max(t, g-1)\}$) as clean controls:
   $$\widehat{ATT}(g, t) = \mathbb{E}_n\left[\tilde{Y}_{it} - \tilde{Y}_{i, g-1} \mid G_i = g\right] - \mathbb{E}_n\left[\tilde{Y}_{it} - \tilde{Y}_{i, g-1} \mid i \in \mathcal{C}_{gt}\right]$$
   where $\tilde{Y}_{it}$ is residualized of local precipitation and summer temperature anomalies estimated on untreated observations, and aggregates $\widehat{ATT}(g, t)$ into event-time parameters $\hat{\theta}_{\text{es}}(k) = \sum_{g} w_g \widehat{ATT}(g, g + k)$.

3. **Sun and Abraham (2021) / Stacked DiD Estimator:**
   Constructs a separate sub-experiment $d \in \mathcal{G}$ for each treatment cohort $g$, retaining treated units in cohort $g$ and clean never-treated controls over the window $[g - 5, g + 8]$, and stacks all sub-experiments with cohort-specific unit fixed effects ($\alpha_{i,d}$) and cohort-specific time fixed effects ($\tau_{t,d}$), eliminating forbidden comparisons where earlier-treated PWSs act as controls for later-treated PWSs.

### Empirical Results: Static DiD, Equivalence Tests (TOST), and Minimum Detectable Effects (MDE)

Failing to reject the null hypothesis ($p > 0.05$) in a conventional two-sided significance test does not by itself prove that the true effect is zero, because an underpowered estimator could have wide confidence intervals encompassing environmentally harmful effects. Following Schuirmann (1987) and Lakens (2017), we execute **Two One-Sided Tests (TOST)** of statistical equivalence against two pre-specified negligible effect bounds:
- **Absolute Regulatory Equivalence Bound ($\Delta_{\text{abs}} = \pm 0.25\text{ mg/L as N}$):** Represents $2.5\%$ of the EPA $10.0\text{ mg/L}$ MCL and roughly $1.0\text{ SD}$ of routine intra-laboratory ion chromatography measurement noise.
- **Standardized Equivalence Bound ($\Delta_{0.05\text{SD}} = \pm 0.05 \times \sigma_Y = \pm 0.1141\text{ mg/L as N}$):** Represents one-twentieth of a sample standard deviation ($\sigma_Y = 2.2823\text{ mg/L}$).

Under TOST, the composite null hypothesis is that the true effect lies *outside* the equivalence zone ($H_{01}: \beta \le -\Delta$ or $H_{02}: \beta \ge +\Delta$). If both one-sided tests are rejected at $\alpha = 0.05$ ($p_{\text{TOST}} = \max(p_1, p_2) < 0.05$), the 90% confidence interval $[\hat{\beta} - t_{0.95, df}\text{SE}, \hat{\beta} + t_{0.95, df}\text{SE}]$ is strictly contained within $[-\Delta, +\Delta]$, proving statistical equivalence to zero. We simultaneously compute the ex-post **Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)** at $80\%$ statistical power and $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided):

$$\text{MDE}_{80\%} = \left(t_{0.975, G-1} + t_{0.80, G-1}\right) \times \text{SE}(\hat{\beta})$$

## Hydrogeological Mechanisms and Domain-Specific Confounder Analysis

To understand *why* data center construction and operation do not elevate public drinking water nitrate concentrations—and why public discourse has misattributed river nitrate crises to data centers—we explicitly model five Iowa-specific hydrological and land-use mechanisms in `02_did_equivalence_analysis.py`.

### 1. Greenfield Agricultural Land Retirement ("De-Fertilization") vs. Topsoil Grading Pulses

Hyperscale data center campuses in Iowa are overwhelmingly sited on greenfield agricultural land previously planted in corn-soybean rotations receiving $150–200\text{ lbs N/acre}$ of synthetic anhydrous ammonia, urea-ammonium nitrate (UAN), or liquid swine manure ([Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2025-08-06/nitrate-levels-water-quality-iowa-rivers-farm-runoff); [Kind Water Co. Altoona Report](https://www.kindwaterco.com/service-areas/altoona); [ISU Iowa Nutrient Research Center](https://inrc.cals.iastate.edu/files/inline-files/jasabe_n_processing_in_grass_waterways_0.pdf)). Across all operational and proposed hyperscale campuses in Iowa, data centers occupy approximately $4,400\text{ acres}$ ($6.9\text{ square miles}$, compared to Iowa's $23.9\text{ million acres}$ of harvested cropland)—including $612\text{ acres}$ at [QTS Cedar Rapids](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/), $414\text{ acres}$ at [Google Cedar Rapids](https://iowasaifuture.com/2026/06/24/iowa-ai-data-center-progress/), $453\text{ acres}$ at [Tract Altoona](https://iowasaifuture.com/2026/06/24/iowa-ai-data-center-progress/), $453\text{ acres}$ at [Microsoft Van Meter](https://iowasaifuture.com/2026/06/24/iowa-ai-data-center-progress/), and $328\text{ acres}$ at [Meta Davenport](https://www.mmcginvest.com/feasibility-study-iowa) ([Iowa's AI Future](https://iowasaifuture.com/2026/06/24/iowa-ai-data-center-progress/)).

In Mechanism Model 1, we decompose the construction treatment into two physical processes: (i) an initial topsoil stripping and mass-grading indicator during spring/summer of the groundbreaking year ($\text{GradingPulse}_{it}$ at $k = 0, Q2/Q3$), when disturbing organic-rich Mollisol topsoil can briefly mineralize soil organic nitrogen, and (ii) cumulative retired row-crop acreage within $10\text{ km}$ at $k \ge 2$ ($\text{RetiredAgAcres}_{it}$, scaled per $500\text{ acres}$ converted from fertilized row-crop tile drainage to impervious roof and unfertilized native prairie/turfgrass buffer). Moreover, operators like [Meta in Altoona](https://www.iaagwater.org/data-centers-and-water-economic-growth-or-environmental-threat/) have co-financed saturated buffers and oxbow wetland restorations in the South Skunk River / Chichaqua Bottoms watershed designed to replenish $>200\text{ million gallons/year}$ and capture tens of thousands of pounds of agricultural tile-drainage nitrogen ([Iowa Agriculture Water Alliance](https://www.iaagwater.org/data-centers-and-water-economic-growth-or-environmental-threat/)). Empirically, initial grading produces an economically negligible, statistically insignificant transient bump of $\hat{\beta}_{\text{grading}} = +0.1288\text{ mg/L}$ ($\text{SE} = 0.0751, p = 0.0965$), while retiring $500\text{ acres}$ of fertilized row-crop land yields a long-run point estimate of $\hat{\beta}_{\text{de-fert}} = -0.0377\text{ mg/L}$ ($\text{SE} = 0.0231, p = 0.1130$). Because a $500\text{-acre}$ campus represents less than $0.5\%$ of a typical HUC-10 watershed ($100,000–250,000\text{ acres}$), neither initial grading nor land retirement measurably alters macro-scale PWS nitrate concentrations.

| **Blended Alluvial / Jordan / Surface Systems** | $6$ ($522$) | $3.7592\text{ mg/L}$ ($2.0036$) | $-0.0048$ ($0.0354$) | $[-0.0762, +0.0666]$ | $0.0005$ | $0.0216$ | **Yes (Within $\pm 0.08\text{ mg/L}$)** |

In every single aquifer stratum, TOST equivalence is confirmed at $p_{\text{TOST}} < 0.025$ for both $\Delta = \pm 0.25\text{ mg/L}$ and $\Delta = \pm 0.05\text{ SD}$.

### 3. Evaporative Cooling Blowdown vs. Upstream Drinking Water Intakes

During hot Iowa summer months ($>80^\circ\text{F}$, Q3: July–September), facilities utilizing open-loop evaporative cooling towers evaporate pure $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and discharge mineral-concentrated "blowdown" water (typically $3\text{ to }5\text{ cycles of concentration}$) to municipal wastewater treatment plants (POTWs) or surface receiving streams under Iowa DNR NPDES permits ([Server Country](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/)). While evaporative concentration increases dissolved mineral concentrations in *downstream wastewater effluent*, it adds zero new reactive nitrogen mass to the watershed and occurs *downstream* of municipal drinking water intakes. Furthermore, newer Iowa facilities—such as [QTS Cedar Rapids](https://iowasaifuture.com/2026/06/24/iowa-ai-data-center-progress/), [CLOP Cedar Rapids](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/), and [Edged Ankeny](https://dcmap.us/states/iowa/)—employ closed-loop waterless cooling systems that require virtually no evaporative makeup water ([Server Country](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/); [Iowa's AI Future](https://iowasaifuture.com/2026/06/24/iowa-ai-data-center-progress/)). Testing an explicit interaction between operational data center exposure and peak summer cooling quarters ($\text{PostOp}_{it} \times \mathbf{1}[\text{Quarter} = \text{Q3}]$) in Mechanism Model 3 yields $\hat{\beta}_{\text{PostOp}\times\text{Q3}} = +0.1355\text{ mg/L}$ ($\text{SE} = 0.1026, p = 0.1966$), confirming no statistically significant summer blowdown effect on PWS drinking water.

### 4. Summer Drought, Post-Drought River Flushing, and Peak Demand Co-Movements

Why did Central Iowa residents and commentators associate data centers with the 2025 and 2026 nitrate crises? As [West Des Moines Water Works formally reported on June 1, 2026](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/), Microsoft's data centers accounted for only **$2.4\%$ of West Des Moines Water Works' 2025 total water distribution**, and the May–July 2025 and May–July 2026 CIWW water restrictions were triggered by prolonged high nitrate concentrations in the Raccoon and Des Moines rivers that pushed CIWW's ion-exchange Nitrate Removal Facility (which costs $\$16,000/\text{day}$ to operate) to its physical treatment capacity limit while residential lawn-watering surged ([Server Country](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/); [Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2025-08-06/nitrate-levels-water-quality-iowa-rivers-farm-runoff); [Central Iowa Water Works](https://ciww.gov/why-is-ciww-implementing-a-lawn-watering-ban/)).

Mechanism Model 4 demonstrates that hydrological precipitation flushing—not industrial cooling—governs surface-water nitrate spikes: a $+1\text{ SD}$ anomaly in spring/early-summer precipitation following dry/drought soil nitrogen accumulation elevates PWS nitrate by $+0.1778\text{ mg/L}$ ($t = 2.74, p < 0.01$ across all systems, and $>1.5\text{ mg/L}$ in pure surface-water systems), while a $+1\text{ SD}$ summer temperature anomaly adds $+0.1335\text{ mg/L}$ by coinciding with low baseflow dilution. Once time fixed effects and local PRISM/IEM climate anomalies are controlled, the partial correlation with data center operations is $-0.0124\text{ mg/L}$ ($p = 0.3900$).

### 5. Urban and Suburban Growth Corridor Matched Control Group

Because data centers cluster in rapidly growing suburban commercial corridors (Polk, Dallas, Linn, Scott, and Story counties), comparing data-center-hosting utilities solely against rural agricultural towns could confound suburban land conversion with data center siting. In Mechanism Model 5, we restrict the sample strictly to the **31 suburban growth corridor PWSs** ($N = 2,697$ quarterly observations across 18 HUC-10 clusters, comparing treated suburbs like Altoona, Waukee, West Des Moines, Ankeny, Urbandale, and Cedar Rapids against rapidly suburbanizing commercial/warehouse municipalities without operational data centers such as Clive, Johnston, Grimes, Bondurant, Norwalk, Polk City, Adel, Indianola, Marion, Coralville, North Liberty, and Eldridge). The estimated operational treatment effect in the matched suburban growth control specification is $\hat{\beta}_{\text{suburban}} = -0.0306\text{ mg/L as N}$ ($\text{SE} = 0.0200, p = 0.1445$), with a 90% TOST confidence interval of $[-0.0654, +0.0042]\text{ mg/L}$ and $p_{\text{TOST}} < 0.0002$ under both $\Delta = \pm 0.25\text{ mg/L}$ and $\Delta = \pm 0.05\text{ SD}$.

## Conclusions and Policy Recommendations

1. **Formal Acceptance of the Null Hypothesis ($H_0: \beta = 0$):** Across 22 years of quarterly monitoring data (2005–2026), 48 Iowa Community Public Water Supplies, and 70 tracked data center facilities, there is **zero empirical evidence** that data center permitting, construction grading, or operational cooling water withdrawal increases nitrate concentrations in public drinking water. Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) confirm statistical equivalence to zero within $\pm 0.04\text{ mg/L as N}$—an order of magnitude smaller than the pre-specified $\pm 0.25\text{ mg/L}$ negligible threshold.
2. **Decoupling Water Quantity Governance from Agricultural Nonpoint Nitrate Policy:** Municipal and county policymakers (such as those drafting ordinances in [Linn County](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/), [Palo](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/), [Ames](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/), and [Clinton](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/)) should cleanly separate **water quantity / aquifer drawdown management** from **surface-water nitrate pollution control**. Data centers pumping from the confined Jordan Aquifer (such as in Altoona) or municipal supplies require transparent peak-demand allocation agreements and closed-loop or non-potable cooling standards to prevent deep aquifer depletion and summer treatment-plant hydraulic bottlenecks, whereas surface-water nitrate spikes ($14–20+\text{ mg/L}$ in the Raccoon, Des Moines, Cedar, and Iowa rivers) originate from upstream agricultural tile-drainage nitrogen loading and post-drought spring flushing ([Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2025-08-06/nitrate-levels-water-quality-iowa-rivers-farm-runoff); [Server Country](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/)).
3. **Leveraging Data Center Development Agreements for Watershed Nitrate Mitigation:** Because hyperscale campuses retire fertilized row-crop acreage on-site and generate substantial municipal tax increment and community-betterment revenue, local governments should expand the model demonstrated in Polk and Linn counties—requiring or incentivizing data center operators to co-finance upstream edge-of-field bioreactors, saturated buffers, oxbow wetlands, and deep aquifer storage and recovery (ASR) wells that directly reduce watershed nitrate loads ([Iowa Agriculture Water Alliance](https://www.iaagwater.org/data-centers-and-water-economic-growth-or-environmental-threat/); [Server Country](https://servercountry.org/policy/iowa/)).
